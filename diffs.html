Every E30 and E34 owner ends up here eventually: does my car have a limited-slip differential, or is it spinning one wheel at a time? This file answers that for the two cars in this garage — and, since the question never really goes away, for every RWD BMW chassis that came after them too.

It's organised by chassis generation, not by every individual market trim — a badge-by-badge list across a century of BMW would run to thousands of rows most of which no factory document survives to confirm. Lock percentages and option codes below are cross-checked against factory literature and long-running enthusiast archives; they can still vary by market and model year, so treat this as a garage reference, not a parts-ordering guarantee — check the diff tag or your VIN before you buy one.

01

Wie es funktioniert

Open vs. LSD

An open differential always sends power to whichever rear wheel spins easiest — cheap, smooth, and useless the moment one tyre loses grip. A limited-slip differential (LSD) uses clutch packs, a viscous coupling, or a gear-driven torque-sensing set to keep some drive going to the wheel that still has grip. BMW's own lock figures — 25%, 40%, and so on — describe how hard that clutch pack bites under power.

Three diff housings cover almost the entire classic range: 168 mm (small, four-cylinder cars), 188 mm (sixes, most M3s up to the E46) and 210 mm (M5s, M6s, and the V8-powered M3s from the E9X onward). Later M cars swap the clutch pack for an Active M Differential — an electronically actuated multi-plate unit that can bias torque before a wheel has even started to slip, which is a genuinely different technology wearing the same name.

The E60 M5 is the one every enthusiast argues about at a meet: BMW's own S85 V10 flagship shipped with an open rear diff, leaning entirely on brake-based traction control to fake the effect of a locker. It's the single biggest LSD surprise in the whole lineup — and the reason an aftermarket diff is near-universal on track-driven E60 M5s.

6er & 8er

The grand tourers ran the same hardware as their sedan siblings, one size up. The E24 shared its diffs with the E28; the E31 shared with the E32/E34; and every 6- and 8-series M-badged car since has followed the M5/M3 pattern of the day, open diff for comfort trims, LSD or Active M Differential once the M badge shows up.

The 7-Series was never about a locking diff — it's the one lineup where BMW has always prioritised smoothness over rear-axle drama. The closest thing to a factory answer was ever the Alpina B7, tuned elsewhere entirely, and the M760i/G70's electronically managed rear axle in recent generations.

06

1er & 2er

The small cars borrowed the M3 family's driveline wholesale rather than inventing something smaller. The 1M Coupe and every M2 since have run a variant of the same clutch-pack or Active M Differential fitted to their contemporary M3/M4 — proof BMW treats a locking diff as non-negotiable once a car wears an M badge, whatever the trunk size.

The F40 1-Series broke the pattern by going front-wheel-drive- based (UKL platform, shared with Mini) — the first 1-Series without a traditional rear diff at all.

4er Reihe

The 4-Series has always been the 3-Series coupe and convertible wearing a separate name plate — split off as its own numeral after the E92. Its diff story is the 3er's story one trim level up, right down to the Active M Differential the M4 has shared with the M3 since the F80/F82 twins launched together in 2014.

None of the X-cars run a traditional clutch-pack LSD outside the M versions. Regular xDrive models rely on a wet multi-plate transfer case to move torque front-to-rear and the brakes to fake a locking axle side-to-side — effective on tarmac, but a world away from the mechanical simplicity in the rest of this file. Only the X3 M, X4 M, X5 M and X6 M get an actual Active M Differential at the rear.

09

i-Reihe

Electric

Electric BMWs sidestep the whole question — there's no mechanical differential to argue about. The i4, i5, i7 and iX split torque between a rear motor (and, on M50/M60/xDrive versions, a front motor too) entirely in software, biasing power wheel-to-wheel through the motor controllers and brakes rather than a clutch pack. The closest thing to an "M diff" is the M50/M60's torque-vectoring calibration, which can send more power to the outside rear wheel exactly the way an Active M Differential would — just without the differential.

Nachrüsten

Retrofit swaps

The garage-favourite fact: 188 mm diffs interchange freely across the E30, E36, E34 and Z3 six-cylinder cars, which is why so many open-diff 320i and 518i cars on the road today are running a 325i or M3's factory LSD dropped straight in. The 210 mm case does the same trick across the E28, E34, E39 and Z8 M5 family. It's the single easiest factory upgrade in the whole back-catalogue — no fabrication, just the right junkyard find and the correct final drive ratio for your gearbox.

Legenden & Spitznamen

Garage folklore
Batmobile

The 3.0 CSL's giant air dam, fender scoops and roof-edge spoiler looked so much like a certain superhero's ride that the nickname stuck — and outlived the car by fifty years.

Shark nose

Collector shorthand for the E12/E24/E28/E30 family look: a forward-leaning nose and quad headlights drawn under one design language across three decades of BMWs.

Bimmer vs. Beemer

"Bimmer" is for the cars — coined by the Boston BMW CCA newsletter in the early 1970s. "Beemer"/"Beamer" belongs to the motorcycles, borrowed from BSA's old racing nickname "Beezer."

The propeller that never was

The roundel is Bavaria's white and blue state colours, nothing more. The spinning-propeller story was a clever 1929 advertisement — BMW itself only debunked its own marketing decades later.

Ring Taxi

Since the mid-1980s, BMW M has sold passenger laps of the Nürburgring in its hottest sedans — most famously an M5 driven by the late Sabine Schmitz.

Alpina, Hartge, AC Schnitzer

Three tuning houses that treated a BMW as something to finish, not modify. Alpina became a certified manufacturer and was folded fully into BMW; Hartge earned its own type-approval before closing; AC Schnitzer wound down its tuning operations in 2026 after nearly forty years.

Drift missile

Cheap, light, rear-wheel drive and mechanically simple — the E30 became the default canvas for garage-built drift cars worldwide, helped along by how easily a 325i or M3's 188 mm LSD drops into a base car's rear end.
